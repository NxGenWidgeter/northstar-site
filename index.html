01 — Before you read anything

The sky,
and what's next.

One row above the cards: a greeting, the temperature, whether rain is coming, and a countdown to the next thing you have to be at. Tap it and twelve hours of rain probability, today's range, sunset and PM2.5 drop over the cards.

Weather is Open-Meteo — no key, no account, no sign-up — and it is cached like the headlines, so a laptop that opens without wifi still knows what the sky was doing.

03 — Headlines

Local. World.
One toggle.

Two scopes behind a single switch, remembered between launches. Local pulls Free Malaysia Today, Malay Mail, Malaysiakini, Berita Harian and three more Malaysian outlets; World pulls BBC, Al Jazeera and Hacker News.

Merged newest-first with at most three items per source, so one feed can't flood the panel. The last good fetch is kept on disk for a week: a start with no network still has headlines, labelled cached, with the age.

Questions

Before you install.

If yours isn't here, ask it in the form below — it reaches the people writing the app.

What does Northstar send, and to whom?

Feed requests and calendar fetches, made by the local service on your machine to the addresses you point it at — plus one weather lookup to Open-Meteo for the place you set. No analytics vendor, no account for the free tier. There is an optional usage summary, and it stays off until you tick a box on first launch; the exact payload is printed before you can agree. Clicking a headline hands the link to your browser and stops there.

What is in the usage summary?

Your OS, desktop and language, the app version, a random install id, how many days it has been opened, counts of eight named actions (folding a card, opening the forecast, switching the theme, adding an event, and so on) and counts of failed fetches by kind. Never headlines, event names, your city, calendar addresses, file paths or your machine's name — there is no field for them. The same panel in the app prints the live JSON and has a Stop and delete button.

What happens with no internet?

The panel still opens. Device health and your own events are local; headlines and the forecast come from the last good fetch, labelled cached with its age, and the cards say so instead of pretending to be current. Your calendars stay readable, they just stop updating.

Which systems does it run on?

Linux, as a GTK window over a local service, and Windows through a per-user installer that carries its own Python and WebView2. macOS has no build yet. The panel is a web view either way, so nothing in the design is tied to one desktop.

Do I need an account?

Nothing. There are no accounts at all — nothing to sign up for, nothing to cancel, and your calendars and events never leave your machine.

How do the tables and rides actually work?

Deep links. Northstar opens OpenTable, Uber, Lyft or whatever you have installed with the details filled in. There are no API keys and no partnerships, so the booking, the payment and the confirmation all happen in those apps.

Why not just pin a browser tab?

A tab wants space and attention. Northstar is a narrow strip on the edge of the screen, the glance strip and four cards at once, no scrolling, and it folds down to just what you watch. It also reads your machine, which a web page can't.
